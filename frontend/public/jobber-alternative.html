Feature Gaps That Force Jobber Users to Seek Alternatives

The most common reasons service businesses leave Jobber — based on conversations with hundreds of companies who have made the switch — fall into five categories that represent fundamental architectural limitations rather than missing features that Jobber might add in a future update.

First: offline capability. Jobber's mobile app requires an internet connection for most functions. Technicians working in basements, rural areas, commercial buildings with poor signal, or underground utilities cannot access job details, update status, or complete work orders when they lose connectivity. For trades where 20-40% of work happens in low-signal environments, this is not a minor inconvenience — it is a fundamental operational limitation that forces technicians to revert to paper notes and manual data entry later, defeating the purpose of having a mobile app.

Second: inventory management. Jobber has no real inventory tracking system. You cannot track parts on individual trucks, set reorder points, manage purchase orders, or see real-time stock levels across your operation. For parts-intensive trades (HVAC, plumbing, electrical, appliance repair), this gap forces you to run a separate inventory system — creating data silos, double-entry requirements, and the inability to automatically calculate true job costs including parts consumed.

Third: payroll integration. Jobber tracks time but does not process payroll. You must export time data to a separate payroll system, reconcile discrepancies manually, and manage two systems that should be one. For companies with hourly technicians, overtime calculations, commission structures, or piece-rate pay, this gap creates hours of weekly administrative work that an integrated system eliminates entirely.

Fourth: scalability beyond 20-30 users. Jobber's interface and architecture were designed for small teams. Companies that grow beyond 20-30 technicians consistently report that Jobber's dispatch board becomes unwieldy, its reporting lacks the depth needed for multi-team management, and its per-user pricing makes growth increasingly expensive. The platform that worked well at 8 technicians becomes a constraint at 25 — forcing an expensive and disruptive platform migration at exactly the moment when operational stability matters most.

Fifth: all-in-one pricing. Jobber's per-user pricing model means that every new hire increases your software cost. This creates a perverse incentive against growth — every technician you add costs $29-$49/month in additional software fees on top of their salary, benefits, and vehicle costs. Platforms with flat-rate or tiered pricing that does not scale linearly with headcount remove this friction and align the software's cost structure with the business's growth objectives.
